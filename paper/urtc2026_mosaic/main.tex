\title{What Are We Measuring? Benchmark Integrity and\\
Zone-Based Reframing for Emotion Recognition\\
in Autistic Children}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Nikhil Mahesh}\IEEEauthorblockA{Pioneer Research}}

\begin{abstract}
Automated emotion recognition is widely proposed as an assistive technology for
autistic children, and recent papers report up to 97.95\% accuracy on public
autism facial-expression benchmarks. Independently collected clinical corpora
report 40--78\% on the same task. We audit the public archive behind many of the
former figures. Re-acquiring it, we find it ships two overlapping dataset trees
sharing 45\% of the smaller tree's images, that 21.4\% of its files are
byte-identical duplicates, and that 119 duplicate groups carry contradictory
emotion labels on pixel-identical images. Holding features, classifier, and
images fixed and varying only the split protocol, five-category balanced
accuracy spans 0.346--0.750 across five defensible readings of the same archive.
Duplicate leakage is not the main driver: a nearest-neighbour classifier that
memorises training data exactly gains only 2.1 points from a split where 80.6\%
of test images have a family member in training, and reaches 0.754 at best, so
contamination cannot produce the published figures. The label contradictions are
sharply asymmetric: 279 of 280 zone-level conflicts fall in negative affect,
against 59.5\% expected under a permutation null preserving class marginals
($p<0.0005$). The archive's labelling is far more consistent than chance overall
yet nearly all of its residual inconsistency lies within negative valence.
Collapsing to three action-oriented Emotion Zones cuts chance-corrected
protocol variance by 29.3\%. We argue for coarser labels and multimodal context
over finer face-only categories. This is a measurement audit, not a deployable
classifier, and not a claim about any child's internal state.
\end{abstract}

benchmark integrity, data leakage, label noise, affective computing, autism

\section{Introduction}
Roughly 1 in 31 eight-year-olds in the United States is diagnosed with autism
spectrum disorder (ASD)~\cite{shaw2025}. Because emotional expression in
autistic people is frequently misread by non-autistic observers---the
\emph{double empathy problem}~\cite{nas2018}---automated emotion recognition is
repeatedly proposed as a communication aid.

The literature on this task is in an unusual state. Papers evaluating on public
autism facial-expression archives report accuracies from
80.0\%~\cite{radocaj2025} to 97.95\%~\cite{hanumantharayappa2026}. Papers
evaluating on independently collected, consented clinical corpora report far
less: 40\% for a commercial analyser on the EMBOA autism
corpus~\cite{kiejdo2025}, 57.95--64.92\% for four commercial classifiers on
parent-reported autistic children~\cite{kalantarian2020}, and 66.43\% for a
classifier trained on typically developing children and tested on autistic
children~\cite{grossard2020}. The gap tracks how the evaluation data were
obtained rather than what the models are.

That correlation has become hard to ignore. Publishers have retracted work built
on scraped autism face datasets, including the source paper for the archive we
audit~\cite{talaat2023retracted} and a facial emotion recognition
paper~\cite{gungor2025retracted}, on the grounds that images were collected from
the internet without documented diagnosis, ethical oversight, or guardian
consent. That established a \emph{provenance} failure. It did not establish
whether the reported numbers were also measurement artifacts.

This paper supplies that measurement. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A quantified integrity audit of a widely used public autism emotion
archive, computed from the vendor's own directory layout (Sec.~\ref{sec:archive}).
\item A protocol ablation holding features, classifier, and images fixed while
varying only the split, plus a nearest-neighbour memorisation ceiling that
bounds what contamination can produce (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item A permutation test showing the label contradictions concentrate in
negative affect far beyond what class marginals predict (Sec.~\ref{sec:zones}).
\item A resulting argument for coarser action-oriented zones and multimodal
context over finer face-only categories (Sec.~\ref{sec:beyond}).
\end{enumerate}

We audit an instrument rather than build one. We make no claim about any child's
internal state, and we treat every label as a filing decision about an image
rather than as ground truth about a person.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Cross-population transfer is established.} Grossard \emph{et
al.}~\cite{grossard2020} ran the controlled experiment on lab-collected video
from 157 typically developing and 36 autistic children with subject-independent
cross-validation, obtaining 82.05\% within-population against 66.43\% across.
Gaya-Morey \emph{et al.}~\cite{gayamorey2026} repeated the design with twelve
CNNs on an adjacent population. We do not claim this finding; we ask whether the
public benchmarks now used to measure it can support the measurement.

\textbf{Benchmark contamination is a mature field.} Kapoor and
Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023} catalogue eight leakage types across 294 affected
papers. Barz and Denzler~\cite{barz2020} found 3.3\% and 10\% duplicate rates in
CIFAR test sets. Northcutt \emph{et al.}~\cite{northcutt2021} showed test-set
label errors reorder model rankings, and Recht \emph{et
al.}~\cite{recht2019} showed that the choice of evaluation set alone moves
reported accuracy substantially. Ramos \emph{et al.}~\cite{ramos2025} give the
taxonomy we adopt: \emph{hard} leakage for identical images across a split,
\emph{soft} for near duplicates, \emph{intra-dataset} for overlap within one
dataset's splits. Adimoolam \emph{et al.}~\cite{adimoolam2026} name
\emph{augmented duplicates}; Apicella \emph{et al.}~\cite{apicella2025}
formalise \emph{synthesis leakage}, where train and test items derive from a
common source under a generating transform. None of this work examines a
clinical affect dataset.

\textbf{Contamination in this archive has been asserted, not measured.} The
authors of the FERAC derivative~\cite{ferac2024} reported ``class overlap and
image duplication'' here, removed duplicates, and dropped two classes as
unrecoverable. They reported no detection method, threshold, duplicate rate,
leakage rate, or effect of cleaning. Two groups have now flagged the same
archive; what remains open is the quantity.

\textbf{Dataset critique and documentation.} Birhane and
Prabhu~\cite{birhane2021} audit large scraped image corpora containing people,
including minors, and argue such datasets are ethically defective at the point
of collection. Gebru \emph{et al.}~\cite{gebru2021} and Paullada \emph{et
al.}~\cite{paullada2021} propose the documentation practices whose absence we
observe here.

\textbf{Why the labels might be the problem.} Barrett \emph{et
al.}~\cite{barrett2019} challenge the premise that emotional state is readable
from facial configuration at all. Keating \emph{et al.}~\cite{keating2026}, from
over 265 million motion-capture points, find autistic and non-autistic
expressions differ in which muscles carry each emotion, concluding the groups
may be ``essentially speaking a different language.'' If expressions are
ambiguous under a non-autistic reading, forcing them into six mutually exclusive
categories should produce contradictions concentrated where categories are
closest.

\section{The Archive}
\label{sec:archive}
We audit the public Kaggle archive associated
with~\cite{talaat2023retracted}.\footnote{Slug
\texttt{fatmamtalaat/autistic-children-emotions-dr-fat\allowbreak ma-m-talaat}.
We name it for reproducibility while noting it distributes images of minors of
disputed provenance; readers should weigh that before downloading.} All 1{,}672
SHA-256 digests recorded in an earlier run of ours are present in the fresh
download, so the audit and the models in Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation} describe the
same bytes. Statistics below come from the vendor's own directory layout, before
any re-splitting: this is what a downstream user receives.

\textbf{Two overlapping datasets ship as one.} The archive contains two
top-level trees (Table~\ref{tab:trees}) with different class balances and
different splits, sharing 323 unique images---45.0\% of the smaller tree. This
plausibly explains a puzzling feature of the literature: published descriptions
of this dataset report its size variously as 348, 830, 1{,}603, and ``2500+''
images, consistent with different papers loading different trees.

\textbf{One file in five is a duplicate.} Of 2{,}258 labelled images only
1{,}774 are distinct by SHA-256; 484 files (21.4\%) are byte-identical copies,
in groups reaching size 5. Perceptual hashing at Hamming distance 4 adds 163
near-duplicate components. A filename suffix (\texttt{\_aug\_N}) identifies
1{,}020 augmentation families, 335 larger than one, the largest containing 9,
so a substantial part of the archive is synthetic variation on fewer source
images---synthesis leakage in the sense of~\cite{apicella2025}. Notably these
families are identifiable from filenames alone, so the duplication was
detectable without any image analysis.

\textbf{Pixel-identical images carry contradictory labels.} Under the vendor's
six categories, 119 byte-identical groups (327 files) contain members filed
under more than one emotion. Establishing this requires no external ground truth
and no annotator panel, only a hash: it is an internal inconsistency of the
artifact, which is a cheaper and stronger claim than label-error detection in
the sense of~\cite{northcutt2021}.

\section{How Much of the Number Is the Protocol?}
\label{sec:ablation}
Contamination matters only if it moves results. We hold the images, the
features, and the classifier fixed and vary \emph{only} how train and test are
separated. Features are frozen \texttt{ViT-B/16}
embeddings~\cite{dosovitskiy2021}; the head is a one-hidden-layer MLP with
epoch-wise early stopping; every protocol runs over ten
seeds~\cite{bouthillier2021}. Because \texttt{surprise} has no zone, it is
dropped from \emph{both} arms, so the categorical arm is a five-way task over
exactly the images the zone arm sees and the two columns differ only in label
granularity.

We add a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier as a \emph{memorisation ceiling}. A
low-capacity probe cannot exploit a leaked duplicate, so on its own it cannot
tell us what an end-to-end fine-tuned network might extract from a contaminated
split. 1-NN is the opposite extreme: it stores the training set exactly and
recovers any byte-identical test twin with certainty, bounding the contamination
effect from above without training anything.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:spread} support three claims and
refute a fourth we expected to make.

\textbf{The reported number is largely a choice.} Across five defensible
protocols on identical images, five-category balanced accuracy spans 0.346 to
0.750---a 40.4-point range attributable entirely to how the archive is read.
Seed dispersion is 2.3--6.3 points, so protocol dominates seed variance by
roughly an order of magnitude. A reader given one number cannot tell where in
that band it sits.

\textbf{Zones are markedly more protocol-stable.} The same protocols span 23.8
points under the zone collapse against 40.4 under five categories. Because the
two label spaces have different chance floors, we compare them chance-corrected
as $(\text{BA}-1/k)/(1-1/k)$: the ranges are 0.357 and 0.505, a \textbf{29.3\%}
reduction in protocol-induced variance. The coarser target is not merely easier;
it is less sensitive to a methodological choice the reader cannot see.

\textbf{Duplicate leakage is real but second-order.} Pooled random leaks the
family of 80.6\% of its test images and exceeds the group-aware split by 5.0
points under five categories and 0.8 under zones. The memorisation ceiling is
stricter still: 1-NN gains only 2.1 and 0.3 points respectively from the same
contamination.

\textbf{Contamination does not explain the published figures.} The highest value
any protocol reaches, under any model including the explicit memoriser, is
0.755. That is 22 points below the 97.95\% reported on this dataset
family~\cite{hanumantharayappa2026}. Since 1-NN upper-bounds what memorising a
contaminated training set can buy, the published figures are not reachable by
duplicate leakage under this feature representation. They must originate
elsewhere---in augmentation applied before splitting in downstream derivatives,
in test sets of a few dozen images, or in selection among many runs. Our audit
constrains the explanation without completing it, and we flag this as the
clearest target for follow-up work.

\section{Where the Contradictions Live}
\label{sec:zones}
The contradictions are not spread evenly. Mapping the vendor's categories onto
three valence/arousal zones---zone~1 (natural, joy), zone~2 (anger, fear),
zone~3 (sadness), with \texttt{surprise} unmapped---and recounting gives

Zones 2 and 3 cover most of the archive, so some concentration is expected for
free. We therefore test against a null that permutes labels across the zoneable
images, preserving both the zone marginals and the duplicate-group structure,
over 2{,}000 replicates. The null puts 59.5\% of conflicted files in negative
affect (95th percentile 61.7\%). The observed share is \textbf{99.6\%}, reached
by no replicate ($p<0.0005$).

The same null is informative in the other direction: it expects 535.8 conflicted
files where the archive has 280. So the archive's label assignments are
\emph{far more consistent than chance overall}, and almost all of the residual
inconsistency lies inside negative valence. Whatever process filed these images
separates positive from negative reliably and fails to separate the negative
categories from one another.

We deliberately do not characterise that process as human annotator
disagreement. The archive is a scrape with no documented labelling procedure,
and a simpler explanation is available: the same image retrieved under two
search queries and written into two folders. Either way the consequence for a
model is identical---the label space as instantiated does not separate negative
categories---and it is the consequence, not the mechanism, that our evidence
supports. What the pattern rules out is that the inconsistency is generic noise.

\section{Beyond Face-Only Categorical Recognition}
\label{sec:beyond}
Two design consequences follow, and one caution.

\textbf{Coarser, action-oriented labels.} If the reproducible distinction is
valence and the irreproducible one is which negative category applies, a label
space built on the reproducible boundary is better founded than one built across
it. This is the argument for Emotion Zones: classes aligned with what a
caregiver would do---de-escalate, engage, comfort---rather than with which
Ekman category a rater would select. On this archive that is not a stylistic
preference but a measured property: the zone boundary is where filing is
consistent (Sec.~\ref{sec:zones}) and where protocol variance falls by 29.3\%
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}). We have not run a caregiver study, so the
\emph{actionability} of the zones remains a design argument; only their
statistical stability is demonstrated here.

\textbf{The caution: never credit the chance floor.} A collapse from six classes
to three raises chance from 1/6 to 1/3, and comparing raw accuracies across the
two silently rewards that. Our own earlier measurements show a commercial
classifier moving from 28.17\% (six categories) to 45.12\% (three zones), an
apparent 16.95-point gain; chance-corrected, the same relabeling is worth 3.88
points. We report chance-corrected figures for every cross-granularity
comparison in this paper for exactly this reason.

\textbf{Context beyond the face.} Even at its cleanest, this task saturates well
below deployment quality: the group-aware ceiling is 0.727 under zones, and
Barrett \emph{et al.}~\cite{barrett2019} argue facial configuration
underdetermines emotional state in principle. The remaining headroom is
unlikely to be in better face classifiers.

This is where multimodal and vision-language approaches earn their place, and
the reason is a data argument rather than an accuracy argument. Training a
face-only autism-specific model requires autism-specific face data, which is
precisely the resource whose acquisition produced the retractions cited above. A
vision-language model brings broad prior knowledge about scenes, activities, and
posture without requiring any autism-specific training corpus, and can supply
the environmental and interaction context a cropped face cannot. Reporting a
zone together with an explanation a caregiver can check is also more auditable
than a category with a confidence score---which matters given that any such
system is, on this evidence, being evaluated on instruments that do not yet
support the claims made for them.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}
This audits one archive and does not establish prevalence, though the
qualitative findings of~\cite{ferac2024} are consistent. Duplicate detection is
exact-hash and perceptual-hash based and will miss re-encoded or cropped
duplicates, so our rates are lower bounds; we did not validate the Hamming-4
threshold against hand-labelled pairs. The archive supplies no subject
identities, so our groups are duplicate and augmentation families, not people;
a subject-disjoint split would be stricter. Our features come from a
web-pretrained backbone, so we cannot exclude that the encoder has previously
seen these images---a caveat that applies to every published result on this
archive and that our ``0\% leakage'' rows do not escape. Finally, the
memorisation ceiling bounds contamination under \emph{these} features; a fully
fine-tuned network was not run.

\textbf{Ethics.} This is secondary analysis of a public dataset with no
interaction with human subjects and no attempt to re-identify anyone; it is not
human-subjects research under 45 CFR 46.102, and no IRB determination was
required. We report only aggregate statistics: no image, filename, hash, or
per-image prediction is released. The image corpus was deleted after hashing and
feature extraction. We make no claim that any depicted child is autistic, that
any label reflects a felt emotion, or that any system should be deployed on this
basis; where Sec.~\ref{sec:zones} references findings about autistic expression,
it does so to motivate a hypothesis, not to assert that this archive depicts
diagnosed children. The archive's provenance is disputed and its license
unstated, so we redistribute nothing.

\section{Conclusion}
A widely used public autism emotion archive ships two overlapping copies sharing
45\% of the smaller, is 21.4\% duplicated, and contains 119 groups of
pixel-identical images with contradictory labels. Varying only the split
protocol moves five-category balanced accuracy across a 40.4-point band, so a
single number on this archive carries little information about a model.
Duplicate leakage is not the main culprit---worth 5.0 points, and only 2.1 to an
explicit memoriser---and no protocol reaches the published figures, which we
therefore cannot attribute to contamination.

The label contradictions concentrate in negative affect at 99.6\% against 59.5\%
expected under a marginal-preserving null, while the archive is otherwise more
self-consistent than chance. The categorical schema, not generic noise, is where
the inconsistency lives, and the three-zone collapse cuts chance-corrected
protocol variance by 29.3\%. Coarser action-oriented zones and multimodal
context are better founded responses than finer face-only categories. Any future
benchmark here should report a duplicate audit, a group-aware split, and
chance-corrected metrics as a condition of being believed---and, given how this
archive was assembled, should not be built by scraping.
